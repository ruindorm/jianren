# Translation Notes

*Bastard Blade* is a **transcreation** of the Chinese original 《劍人》, not a literal translation. Comedy does not survive word-for-word rendering. This page records what was changed and why — partly for readers, partly so future translators into other languages know which jokes are load-bearing.

## The Title

The Chinese title 劍人 (*jiàn rén*, "sword person") is a homophone of 賤人 (*jiàn rén*, "bastard / scumbag"). The entire title is a pun, and it is untranslatable.

**Bastard Blade** reconstructs the same double meaning through a different route: the blade *is* a bastard, and it is used to cut down bastards. The tagline — *Slay the bastards. My sword is for picking up girls.* — carries the original's second half intact.

## Jokes That Had to Be Rebuilt

### "That's right" → "no cap"

In the original, Male Lead says the English phrase *that's right*, transliterated into Chinese characters as 辣死賴. Master, who speaks no English, is baffled and delivers a solemn lesson about not clinging to names. Junior Sister then "explains" it wrongly but confidently.

In English, Master obviously understands *that's right*. The joke is dead on arrival. It was rebuilt around **"no cap"** — modern slang an elderly cultivator plausibly would not know. The structure survives exactly: Master doesn't understand it, refuses to be bothered by it, and Junior Sister redeploys it enthusiastically.

### "you bitch" → "be… beach!"

In the original, Gate Boy mishears *you bitch* as the Chinese transliteration 優閉取, then repeats it as "be… 酷" (*be cool*). The humour is a child innocently parroting an obscenity he can't parse.

English keeps Gate Boy's question ("what does 'you bitch' mean?") and rebuilds the payoff as **"be… beach!"** — the same innocent mangling, the same beat.

## What Translated Cleanly

The technology jokes needed almost no adaptation, and are arguably stronger in English:

> configure a git environment first — so the results can be rolled back
> add that question to the search union
> according to my historical win-rate analysis
> executing contingency plan two — fallback path
> open book exam. Don't blame me when you still fail

These land identically in any language with software engineers in it.

## Names

Personal names are transliterated: **Qin Feiyu**, **Long Aotian**, **Yuan Ping**, **Yun'er**.

**Long Aotian** (龍傲天) is kept in pinyin for a further reason. In Chinese internet culture the
name is shorthand for an insufferably invincible protagonist — roughly the Gary Stu — and the
joke is that he is the *antagonist* here. Translating it would erase that.

**Wu Ming** (吾名) is the protagonist's name, and it is a problem. In Chinese it does not read as
a name at all: 吾名 literally means *my name*, and it is pronounced identically to 無名,
*nameless*. When he is forced to declare himself at the Sword Gate, what he shouts is, in effect,
"My name: My Name!" — which is why everyone present responds with question marks.

Transliterating alone would kill that scene: "My name: Wu Ming!" is a perfectly ordinary
declaration, and the confusion of the onlookers would make no sense. Translating alone would
break with every other name in the book.

So the book does both. He is **Wu Ming** throughout, and at the moment of declaration the line
plays in English with a one-line gloss of the pronunciation. Readers who follow translated
fiction will recognise the device; the effect is not identical to the Chinese, but it is not lost
either.

**Male Lead** and **Sword** are left as labels rather than names because that is what they are in
the original — the story breaks the fourth wall repeatedly (the sword mentions "the audience"),
and the placeholder labels are part of the joke.

## Register

The original is crude. The translation is crude. Softening the profanity would flatten the protagonist into someone he is not — his defining trait is that he is a foul-mouthed idiot with an even more foul-mouthed sword.
